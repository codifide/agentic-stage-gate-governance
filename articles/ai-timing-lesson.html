The $200K Lesson

Why AI Timing Matters More Than Strategy — A Medical Records Automation Story

Douglas Jones · May 2026

The Problem

It's 3:47 PM on a Tuesday. A Release of Information (ROI) processor stares at her screen. A new fax just arrived — an authorization form from a provider requesting medical records. She opens the scanned image and sighs. It's a photocopy of a photocopy, skewed at a 15-degree angle, with handwritten notes scrawled across printed text. Some fields are barely legible.

Before any medical records can be retrieved, she must manually decipher and enter more than 50 fields: patient name, date of birth, address, the facility releasing records, the facility receiving them, 20+ checkboxes for record types, sensitive information flags, date ranges, delivery methods, and signature verification. If she misreads a single digit or transposes numbers in a fax number, the entire request could be delayed or routed incorrectly.

This isn't an edge case. This is Tuesday.

Welcome to Release of Information processing — the invisible infrastructure that keeps healthcare moving. When insurance companies need records to approve a claim, when hospital systems need patient history for treatment decisions, when government entities conduct audits — they all depend on ROI processors to get the right records to the right place at the right time. Patient care outcomes are directly tied to how fast these records move.

At a large healthcare services organization, over 1,000 people work on data capture alone. Every authorization form that arrives via fax — and most still do — requires human interpretation before it enters the digital workflow. At a baseline cost of $3–4 per authorization for manual processing, the operational stakes are significant. More importantly, every minute spent deciphering a faded photocopy is a minute a patient waits for care.

The challenge isn't just volume. It's the nature of the documents themselves. Authorization forms arrive as photocopies of photocopies, skewed scans, faded faxes, and handwritten notes overlaying printed forms. Thousands of providers use inconsistent formats. The documents are uniquely difficult for both humans and machines to read.

The First Attempt: The Vendor

In August 2023, the organization discovered a vendor promising AI-powered document intelligence. The pitch was compelling: their system could read even the most degraded authorization forms, extract structured data, and feed it directly into the workflow. The initial proposal was $1 per authorization. Compared to the $3–4 manual processing cost, the ROI looked solid.

The deal was signed. This wasn't a small pilot. Security, Legal, Infrastructure, and Finance were engaged. Formal governance was established. The vendor's infrastructure was substantial: Kubernetes, PostgreSQL, Elasticsearch, graph databases, and ML libraries. Standard SDLC practices were mandated.

But challenges emerged. The system struggled with documents exceeding 10 pages. Functional constraints remained unresolved. Requests for ETAs went unanswered. And then there was the cost issue.

As early as the initial discussions, internal leadership had flagged pricing concerns. As the project progressed, those concerns proved prescient. The actual infrastructure costs weren't just higher than the initial proposal — they were orders of magnitude higher. The economics collapsed.

After approximately 10 months and over $200K in upfront vendor costs plus supporting infrastructure spend, leadership made the call to pause the project. Had the vendor solution actually reached production at their proposed pricing and infrastructure requirements, the projected annual operating cost would have exceeded $1 million per year — for a single document processing workflow.

The Learning Opportunity

It would be easy to call this a failure. But that's not quite right. The vendor wasn't incompetent. The team wasn't unprepared. The problem was simpler and more fundamental: the technology wasn't ready yet.

In 2023 and early 2024, vision AI models couldn't reliably handle degraded, complex documents at this scale. OCR struggled with handwriting overlays. Context understanding was limited. And the cost of running custom AI infrastructure at scale was prohibitive.

More importantly, the team learned what "production-ready" really meant for this use case:

  • Which document features were hardest to extract
  • Where confidence scoring mattered most
  • What the staff actually needed from an AI system
  • What integration with existing workflows required

These lessons proved invaluable when we tried again.

Getting Buy-In After a High-Profile Pause

The hardest part wasn't the technical decision — it was organizational. How do you convince leadership to invest in AI again after a $200K pause?

The answer: patience, evidence, and small bets. No big budget requests. Small proof-of-concepts on personal time. Tracking AI industry progress. Sharing incremental results. When the POC crossed the threshold, there was evidence, not promises. That made all the difference.

The narrative was reframed: the vendor engagement wasn't a "failure" — it was reconnaissance. The organization learned what didn't work in 2023. That knowledge made everyone smarter in 2025.

The Breakthrough: Platform AI

Between June 2024 and October 2025, the team watched and tested. The AI landscape was evolving rapidly. Foundation models were maturing. Vision-language models that could understand both images and text were getting better at degraded documents, handwriting recognition, and contextual understanding. These weren't incremental improvements — they were step-function changes in capability.

More importantly, the economics were shifting. Managed AI platforms launched with pay-per-use pricing. No custom infrastructure. No surprise costs. Just API calls with predictable pricing.

In October 2025, something changed. Another internal proof-of-concept. This time, the results crossed the threshold. Confidence scores achieved >90%. The cost model made sense. It wasn't perfect — no AI system is — but it was production-viable.

The difference between June 2024 and October 2025? Eighteen months of AI evolution.

Building It Internally

The team was small:

  • 1 Solution Architect (part-time)
  • 2 Backend Engineers (full-time)
  • 1 QA Engineer (part-time)
  • 1 Product Manager (part-time)
  • Domain experts from ROI operations (advisory)

Timeline: 5 months from POC approval to production deployment — a fraction of the vendor engagement.

The Architecture

At the core: a RESTful API with over 20 endpoints orchestrating the entire process. It integrates with the existing health data system, manages configuration in a PostgreSQL database, and calls a managed vision-language model for document understanding.

The architecture supports multi-client deployment with per-client configuration, confidence-based routing, and full observability into every request.

Prompt Engineering as Systems Engineering

This isn't "call an API and hope for the best." The heart of the system is a sophisticated, database-driven prompt engineered to extract structured data from chaotic documents:

  • Patient demographics: Name, DOB, address, phone, email
  • FROM facility: The facility releasing records — name, address, phone, fax
  • RECIPIENT facility: The facility receiving records — name, address, phone, fax
  • Record types: 20+ checkboxes with strict true/false logic
  • Sensitive information flags: Genetic testing, HIV, behavioral health, substance abuse
  • Time periods: Single dates, ranges, or relative periods
  • Signature verification: Handwritten signatures vs. printed names

The prompt is stored in the database and fully configurable. Adjusting extraction logic requires a database update — no code deployment. This is prompt engineering as a discipline, not an afterthought.

Patient Safety Controls

When you're dealing with medical records, patient safety isn't optional — it's foundational:

Confidence Level Action
HIGH (>85%) Auto-update workflow, logged for audit
MEDIUM (70–85%) Flag for human review before updating
LOW (<70%) Route to manual processing entirely

The key insight: AI augments human judgment, it doesn't replace it. Processors are faster and less fatigued, but they remain the final authority on data quality. No patient harm has occurred due to AI error — the safeguards work.

Key Lessons

1. Timing Is Everything

AI technology maturity matters more than strategy, budget, or vendor selection. In 2023, vision AI wasn't ready for this use case. By late 2025, it was. The team didn't get smarter. The technology got better.

Critical implication: Don't write off "failed" AI projects forever. If you shelved an AI initiative 2–3 years ago because the technology wasn't ready, it's time to revisit it. The AI landscape has changed dramatically.

2. Platform Maturity Changed the Economics

Managed AI platforms fundamentally changed the build-vs-buy equation. No training custom models. No managing ML infrastructure. Just API calls with predictable, pay-per-use pricing. What used to require a team of ML engineers and expensive infrastructure now requires prompt engineering and systems integration.

3. Prompt Engineering Is Systems Engineering

The system isn't just "call an API and parse the response." It's database-driven configuration, multi-client support, confidence-based routing, integration with existing workflows, and human-in-the-loop quality controls. The prompt itself encodes domain knowledge, handles edge cases, enforces data quality rules, and adapts to document variations.

4. Patient Safety Must Be Foundational

Confidence thresholds, human-in-the-loop protocols, critical field validation, and error handling aren't nice-to-haves. They're requirements. AI should augment human judgment, not replace it.

5. Internal Development Became Viable

With the vendor, the organization was dependent on their roadmap, pricing, and infrastructure. With the internal build, the team controls the iteration cycle — prompts, features, bugs, scale, all on their own timeline. Foundation models made this possible.

6. Change Management Matters as Much as Technology

Getting organizational buy-in after a high-profile pause was harder than the technical work. The keys: patience, evidence-based decision making, small bets, and reframing "failure" as learning.

When to Build vs. Buy in 2026

Build internally when:

  • You have engineering talent comfortable with APIs and prompt engineering
  • Your use case is specific to your workflows and data
  • You need fast iteration and control
  • Platform AI covers your capability needs
  • You have operational discipline for maintenance and support

Buy from a vendor when:

  • You need capabilities beyond foundation models (custom training, specialized models)
  • You lack internal engineering capacity
  • The vendor offers domain expertise you don't have
  • You need enterprise SLAs and 24/7 support
  • The vendor's roadmap aligns with your needs
The governance connection

This project demonstrates a recurring pattern: the hard part of AI in production isn't the AI call. It's the systems engineering around it — confidence routing, human-in-the-loop protocols, error handling, observability, and continuous improvement. The same Agentic SDLC Governance principles that govern autonomous coding loops apply to autonomous document processing: gate what requires judgment, loop what can be verified.
